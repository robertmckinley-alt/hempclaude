The 3AM problem, and the 78 days left to solve it

Falling asleep was never the hard part. Staying asleep is — and the category of product that finally cracked it for a lot of people is about to be regulated off the shelf.

There is a specific kind of tired that comes from waking at three in the morning. Not the tired of a late night, which at least feels earned. This is the tired of having done everything right — in bed by eleven, phone in the other room, room cold and dark — and finding yourself awake anyway, four hours later, with the whole of the next day already ruined and nothing to do but wait for it.

Roughly a third of American adults report regular sleep problems, and a meaningful share of them describe exactly this pattern: sleep onset is fine, sleep maintenance is not. It is a distinction that matters enormously in practice and gets flattened in almost every conversation about sleep aids, because the most common intervention — melatonin — is fundamentally an onset drug. It tells your body that it is night. It does very little to keep you under once you are there.

Which is why, over the past several years, a lot of people quietly migrated to something else.

What changed in the cupboard

The 2018 Farm Bill legalised hemp by defining it as cannabis containing no more than 0.3% delta-9 THC by dry weight. That definition — specifically the word delta-9 — opened a lane that nobody in Congress had especially intended, and an entire consumer category grew inside it. Among the most popular products to emerge were sleep formulas built on a stack rather than a single ingredient: melatonin for onset, CBN for physical sedation, and a low dose of THC for the part of the night where the others give out.

People who had cycled through melatonin, antihistamines, and prescription options found that this combination did something the others did not. It is not that it is stronger. It is that it targets a different failure point.

Melatonin is an onset drug. If your problem is at 3am, you have been treating the wrong half of the night.

That lane is now closing.

What the law does

On November 12, 2025, Public Law 119-37 was signed. Section 781 of it rewrites the federal hemp definition in two ways. First, it changes the measured compound from delta-9 THC to total THC, folding in THCA and delta-8. Second — and this is the one that reaches the consumer shelf — it caps finished products at 0.4 milligrams of total THC per container.

To understand what that means in practice, it helps to do the arithmetic on an ordinary product. A standard sleep gummy carries 5mg of THC and comes twenty to a bag. That is 100 milligrams per container, against a cap of 0.4.

The cap is written per container, not per serving, which forecloses the obvious workaround. A brand cannot simply lower the dose and keep the count — twenty gummies sharing 0.4mg comes to 0.02mg each, which is not a dose of anything. There is no compliant version of an intoxicating hemp product at that threshold. The category does not shrink; it ends.

What is not being banned

It is worth being precise here, because the coverage has not been. The law caps THC. It does not ban hemp, and it does not touch the rest of the sleep stack.

  • CBN is unaffected. Cannabinol is not a THC isomer and is not covered by the cap. The ingredient most responsible for the physical, heavy-limbed quality of these formulas continues to be legal.
  • CBD is unaffected, provided the finished product stays under the 0.4mg total THC threshold.
  • Melatonin is a dietary supplement and sits entirely outside the scope of this law.

So a CBD + CBN + melatonin sleep formula survives November perfectly well. What does not survive is the THC component — and for the people for whom that component was the thing that finally worked, that is the whole question.

The practical decision

If you use one of these products and it works, you have two sensible options and about eleven weeks to act on them. You can buy what you want at current pricing while the current formulations are still being manufactured. Or you can trial a THC-free formulation now, while both versions still exist and you can compare them directly — which is a considerably better position than trying to find a replacement in December.

Doing both is not unreasonable either. What is unreasonable is assuming the date moves. The proposed delay is a bill that has passed one chamber, and bills that have passed one chamber fail all the time.

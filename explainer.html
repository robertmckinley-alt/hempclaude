The short version

  • The federal definition of hemp changes from delta-9 THC only to total THC — which folds in THCA, delta-8, and delta-10.
  • Finished consumer products get a hard cap of 0.4 milligrams of total THC per container.
  • A typical 5mg × 20-count sleep gummy contains 100mg per container — roughly 250× the new limit.
  • The U.S. Hemp Roundtable estimates this makes about 95% of existing hemp cannabinoid products federally unlawful.
  • Effective date is November 12, 2026. A delay to December 11 passed the Senate on August 8 but has not been enacted.
  • CBD, CBN, and melatonin are not banned. Only the THC content is capped.

For eight years, the hemp industry operated inside a definition written in the 2018 Farm Bill: hemp was cannabis containing no more than 0.3% delta-9 THC by dry weight. That single word — delta-9 — is what made the modern hemp cannabinoid market possible. It is also the word Congress just removed.

What actually changed

On November 12, 2025, the President signed the Continuing Appropriations Act into law as Public Law 119-37. Inside it, at Section 781, was a rewrite of the federal hemp definition that the hemp industry had been fighting off since 2024.

The change is small on paper and enormous in practice:

 2018 Farm Bill (current)P.L. 119-37 §781 (from Nov 12)
Measured compound Delta-9 THC only Total THC — including THCA, delta-8, delta-10
Crop threshold 0.3% by dry weight 0.3% by dry weight (same number, far broader measure)
Finished-product cap None 0.4mg total THC per container
Synthetic cannabinoids Grey area, widely sold Excluded from the definition

Switching from delta-9 to total THC closes what the industry called the THCA loophole. THCA is the non-intoxicating acid form of THC that converts to delta-9 when heated — so a flower product could test under 0.3% delta-9 while being, functionally, high-THC cannabis. Under a total-THC standard, that arithmetic no longer works.

But the provision that reaches furthest into the consumer market is the second one: the per-container cap.

The math that decides everything

0.4 milligrams is a very small number. It is small enough that it is worth walking through what it means for an ordinary product on an ordinary shelf. Take a standard hemp-derived sleep gummy — 5mg of THC per piece, twenty pieces in the bottle:

A 10mg version of the same product lands at 200mg per container — 500× the cap. The gap is not a formulation problem that can be reformulated around. At 0.4mg per container, a compliant product cannot contain a meaningful dose of THC at all.

This is the detail that gets lost in most coverage. The cap is written per container, not per serving. A brand cannot comply by shrinking the dose per gummy and keeping the count — twenty gummies sharing 0.4mg works out to 0.02mg each. There is no version of an intoxicating hemp product that survives this threshold.

How it got here, and what could still change

December 2018

The 2018 Farm Bill legalises hemp

Defines hemp by delta-9 THC content at 0.3% dry weight. The modern hemp cannabinoid market is built on this definition.

November 12, 2025

P.L. 119-37 is signed

Section 781 of the Continuing Appropriations Act (H.R. 5371) rewrites the definition and adds the 0.4mg per-container cap — with a one-year runway before it bites.

August 8, 2026

Senate passes a delay to December 11

A stopgap funding bill would push the effective date back by roughly a month. It still requires House passage and a signature. It is not law.

Today · August 26, 2026

78 days remain

November 12 remains the operative date. Retailers and manufacturers are working to the enacted deadline, not the proposed one.

November 12, 2026

The new standard takes effect

Absent intervening legislation, products above the cap fall outside the federal hemp definition.

A word about the December 11 date

You will see both November 12 and December 11 cited. Only one of them is law. The December date comes from a Senate-passed stopgap that has not cleared the House or been signed. Treating it as settled is a bet on a bill that has not passed — and if it fails, the extra month simply does not exist.

What is not going away

The coverage of this law has been sloppy, and a lot of people have come away believing hemp is being banned outright. It is not. The statute caps THC. It does not touch the other cannabinoids, and it does not touch conventional sleep ingredients.

Still federally lawful after November 12

CBD — unaffected, provided the finished product stays under 0.4mg total THC per container. CBN (cannabinol) — the cannabinoid most associated with sedation in sleep formulas — is not a THC isomer and is not capped. Melatonin — a dietary supplement, entirely outside the scope of this law.

In practice this means a CBD + CBN + melatonin sleep formula can continue to be sold. What disappears is the THC component.

What it means if you currently use a THC sleep product

  • Product you already own remains yours. The law governs what may be produced and sold, not what is sitting in your cupboard.
  • Supply gets tight before the date, not on it. Manufacturers stop production runs well ahead of a deadline they cannot sell into. Expect thinning inventory through October rather than a clean cutoff.
  • State law is a separate question. Several states already restrict these products independently. Federal legality is a floor, not a guarantee.
  • THC-free formulations are the continuity path. If the CBN and melatonin are doing meaningful work in your routine, a THC-free version of the same formula is not affected by any of this.

The practical read

If you use these products and they work for you, there are really only two decisions in front of you between now and November. The first is whether to buy what you want at current prices while the current formulations are still being made. The second is whether to trial a THC-free formulation now, while you can still compare the two directly, rather than after one of them is gone.

Both are reasonable. Neither requires panic. What is not reasonable is assuming the date will move — the delay is a proposal, and proposals fail.
